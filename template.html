What the satellites are actually seeing

Each complex is classified by how it behaves across the week — how intense it becomes, how far it spreads, and whether it keeps reappearing from one fixed point. A gas flare and a forest fire both register as heat. They do not behave alike.

How to read this, and what it does not say

Not an emergency product

This is an analytical view of satellite thermal detections, not a fire warning service. It is unvalidated, not continuous and not authoritative. For operational information use the European Forest Fire Information System, your national civil protection service, or the emergency number 112.

The classification is a heuristic

It takes no land-cover input, so an intense agricultural fire can be labelled wildfire and a slow-burning peat fire can be labelled a persistent heat source. It was checked against known geography — Algerian gas flaring, Ukrainian crop residue, Iberian and Greek wildfire — and behaves sensibly on all four. That is evidence of usefulness, not proof of correctness.

Detections are not fires

A 375 m pixel flags a thermal anomaly. One fire generates many detections across many overpasses; clustering reduces the double-counting but cannot eliminate it.

Radiative power is not burned area

FRP measures energy emitted at the instant of overpass. It indicates intensity, not extent. Burned area needs post-fire optical mapping — Sentinel-2 dNBR — or the official EFFIS perimeters.

Gaps are invisible

Smoke and cloud block detection, and each satellite passes roughly twice daily, so a fire that ignited and was contained between overpasses never appears. Absence of detections is not evidence of absence of fire.

Coverage

The frame spans 25°W–40°E, 33–64°N, excluding % of detections, which are still counted in every figure. Seven days is the cap on NASA's open feeds.
